### 1. Irrationality of √2

**Informal.** √2 is irrational.

**Scope.** `Irrational(√2)`, where `Irrational(x)` says that no rational `q ∈ ℚ` is sent to `x` by the embedding `rationalCut` of ℚ into ℝ: the textbook `√2 ∉ ℚ`, with ℚ read as its copy inside ℝ. The set form `√2 ∉ rationalReals` (the image of the embedding), the pointwise form `√2 ≠ rationalCut(q)`, and the quotient form `√2 ≠ p/q` for naturals `p` and `q ≠ 0` are stated as well. The proof shows the embedding is additive, carries multiplication by a natural, and sends a rational (a − b)/m to a real with `rationalCut(q)·m = a − b`; squaring reduces to the natural-number statement that no `p` and positive `q` satisfy `p² = 2q²`, proved by descent from the least such `p`.

### 2. Fundamental theorem of algebra

**Informal.** Every nonconstant complex polynomial has a complex root.

**Scope.** Root existence for every nonconstant polynomial over the concrete Dedekind-real complex field: in the monic form `complexes.fundamental_theorem_of_algebra`, in the general form with an explicit nonzero leading coefficient (`hornerAt` with lower coefficients `cs ≠ []`), and in the single-coefficient-list form where some coefficient of index at least one is nonzero. The general forms divide by the leading coefficient and reduce to the monic case. The proof inherits Global Choice through minimum attainment. Counting roots with multiplicity is a separate theorem, not proved here.

### 10. Euler's generalization of Fermat's little theorem

**Informal.** If gcd(a, n) = 1 then a^φ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n); for a prime p and p ∤ a, a^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p).

**Scope.** For naturals `a` and `n > 1` with `gcd(a, n) = 1`, `a^φ(n) % n = 1`, where `φ(n)` is the length of the list of residues below `n` coprime to `n`. The proof scales that list by `a`: the scaled entries have the same multiplicities as the original list (an inverse of `a` exists by a counting argument on residues), so their products agree, and the product of units is coprime to `n`, so it cancels. Fermat's little theorem `a^(p−1) % p = 1` for a prime `p ∤ a` follows from `φ(p) = p − 1`. The totient is defined by this count rather than by a product formula, and its multiplicativity is not proved.

[Proof: euler.theorem](../lib/Grace/Cumulus/NumberTheory/Euler.g17#L118)

### 15. Fundamental theorem of integral calculus

**Informal.** For continuous f, differentiating x ↦ ∫ₐˣ f recovers f. A continuous derivative integrates to the endpoint difference.

**Scope.** Riemann integration on real closed intervals. FTC-I is an interior derivative statement; the second displayed theorem assumes a continuous integrand and the stated local derivative hypotheses. No Lebesgue-integral generality is claimed.

[Proof: FTC.derivative_of_integral](../lib/Grace/Cumulus/Real/Analysis/Integration/FTCFirst.g17#L137)

### 23. Formula for Pythagorean triples

**Informal.** The primitive Pythagorean triples are exactly (m² − n², 2mn, m² + n²) with m > n > 0 coprime and of opposite parity, up to the order of the legs; every Pythagorean triple is a multiple of a primitive one.

**Scope.** A Pythagorean triple is a triple of positive naturals with `a ^ 2 + b ^ 2 = c ^ 2`, primitive when `gcd(a, b) = 1`; "opposite parity" is `Odd(m + n)`. Both directions are proved over the naturals: `pythagorean.of_parameters` and `pythagorean.primitive_of_parameters` give the triples of the parametrization, `pythagorean.one_even` shows a primitive triple has exactly one even leg, and `pythagorean.primitive_form` recovers `m` and `n` for every primitive triple with `b` even, so every primitive triple up to swapping the legs. `pythagorean.form` writes any triple as `gcd(a, b)` times a primitive one. The arithmetic heart, that coprime factors of a square are squares, is `IsSquare.of_coprime_mul`, proved by the cofactor argument on `gcd(a, c)` without prime factorization. Everything is in ZF.

[Proof: pythagorean.primitive_form](../lib/Grace/Cumulus/NumberTheory/PythagoreanTriples.g17#L228)

<details open>
<summary>Formal Grace17 statement</summary>

<!-- cumulus-source: lib/Grace/Cumulus/NumberTheory/PythagoreanTriples.g17 -->
```g17
theorem pythagorean.primitive_form (a b c : Nat)
  requires primitive : PrimitivePythagorean(a, b, c)
  requires even : Even(b)
  shows ∃ m, Nat(m) ∧ ∃ n, Nat(n) ∧
    0 < n ∧ n < m ∧ gcd(m, n) = 1 ∧ Odd(m + n) ∧
    a = m ^ 2 - n ^ 2 ∧ b = 2 * m * n ∧ c = m ^ 2 + n ^ 2
```

### 25. Schröder–Bernstein Theorem

**Informal.** If there are injections from A into B and from B into A, then there is a bijection between A and B.

**Scope.** Sets A and B with injective functions as graphs in both directions, written A ≼ B and B ≼ A. The bijection is built from the back-and-forth chain: f on the points of A reached from the complement of the image of g by iterating g ∘ f, and the inverse of g elsewhere.

[Proof: Equipotent.of_mutual_dominance](../lib/Grace/Cumulus/Cardinal/Dominance.g17#L389)

theorem Equipotent.of_mutual_dominance (A B : Set)
  requires hAB : A ≼ B
  requires hBA : B ≼ A
  shows A ≃ B

### 30. The ballot problem

**Informal.** If A receives a votes and B receives b < a votes, the orderings of the ballots in which A is strictly ahead throughout the count are (a − b)/(a + b) of all orderings.

**Scope.** A ballot is 0 (a vote for A) or 1 (a vote for B), an ordering is a list over 2 read in the order of the count, `ballots(n)` is the set of such lists of length `n`, and `ballotSequences(a, b)` the lists of length `a + b` with `a` zeros along which every nonempty prefix holds more zeros than ones (`AlwaysAhead`). `ballot.count` gives `(a + b) * cardinality(ballotSequences(a, b)) = (a - b) * ((a + b) choose a)` for `b < a`, from the additive form `ballotSequences.count_succ`, `cardinality(ballotSequences(a + 1, b)) + ((a + b) choose (a + 1)) = (a + b) choose a` for `b ≤ a`, and the absorption identities of the binomial coefficients. The proof is the recursion by the first ballot: the orderings with `a` zeros and `b` ones along which A stays ahead by more than `k` split, by their first ballot, into the cons images of the families at `(k + 1, a − 1, b)` and `(k − 1, a, b − 1)`, and `leadSequences.closed_form` verifies `cardinality + (n choose (a + k + 1)) = n choose a` by induction on `n = a + b` with Pascal's rule; the ballot sequences are the case of a first ballot 0 followed by a lead above one. `orderings.count` gives `cardinality(orderings(a, b)) = (a + b) choose a` for all orderings, and `ballot.probability` the probability form `prob(uniformWeight(orderings(a, b)), ballotSequences(a, b)) = realNat(a - b) / realNat(a + b)` on the uniform space of all orderings. The counting module is in ZF.

### 31. Ramsey's theorem

**Informal.** For every n, every infinite set S, and every colouring of the n-element subsets of S with finitely many colours, some infinite subset of S has all its n-element subsets the same colour; and among any six people, three are mutual acquaintances or three are mutual strangers.

**Scope.** The infinite Ramsey theorem in full: for every natural `n`, every infinite set `S`, and every function `C` from the n-element subsets of `S` into a finite set of colours, there is an infinite `H ⊆ S` and a colour `c` with `C(U) = c` for every n-element subset `U` of `H` (`ramsey.infinite_colours`); `ramsey.infinite` is the two-colour form and `ramsey.infinite_numeral` the form for a numeral of colours. ### 35. Taylor's theorem

**Informal.** If f is n + 1 times differentiable on [a, b], then f(b) = ∑_{k ≤ n} f⁽ᵏ⁾(a)(b − a)ᵏ/k! + f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾(ξ)(b − a)ⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1)! for some ξ strictly between a and b.

**Scope.** The Lagrange remainder form on a closed interval `[A, B]` with `A < B`, for `(f : ℝ → ℝ)`. The derivative is the function `f′` chosen from `HasDerivativeAt`, and `f⁽ᵏ⁾` is that derivative applied `k` times; the hypothesis is `∀ k ≤ n, ∀ X ∈ closedInterval(A, B), DifferentiableAt(f⁽ᵏ⁾, X)`, and the conclusion is written as on paper, `f(B) = ∑ k ≤ n, f⁽ᵏ⁾(A) * (B - A) ^ k / k! + f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾(C) * (B - A) ^ (n + 1) / (n + 1)!`, the factorials read as reals and the sum being the library's `∑` over the term sequence `taylorTerms(f, A, B)`. Proved for every `n` by induction on the derivative of the Taylor approximant and the Cauchy mean value theorem; the `n = 0` instance is checked to read as the mean value theorem, `f(B) = f(A) + f′(C) * (B - A)`. The case `B < A` is not stated separately.

[Proof: Taylor.theorem](../lib/Grace/Cumulus/Real/Analysis/Taylor.g17#L16)

### 37. Solution of a cubic

**Informal.** Cardano's formula: the roots of a cubic are given by radicals.

**Scope.** Cardano's formula over the concrete Dedekind-real complex field. ### 44. The binomial theorem

**Informal.** (a + b)ⁿ = Σₖ C(n, k) aᵏ bⁿ⁻ᵏ.

**Scope.** For real `A`, `B` and every natural `n`, `(A + B)^n` equals the partial sum over `k < n + 1` of the terms `choose(n, k)·A^k·B^(n − k)`, with the binomial coefficient defined by Pascal's rule and the exponent `n − k` as truncated subtraction. **Informal.** Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial.

### 51. Wilson's theorem

**Informal.** (p − 1)! ≡ −1 (mod p) for every prime p.

**Scope.** Both directions. The residues `2, …, p − 2` are paired with their modular inverses, chosen by uniqueness: a residue is its own inverse only when it is `1` or `p − 1`, so the product of the middle residues is `1` modulo `p` by induction on the length of such paired lists, and the remaining factor `p − 1` contributes `−1`; the case `p = 2` is computed directly. For the converse, a proper divisor `d` of `n` divides `(n − 1)!` and hence `1`.

### 57. Heron's formula

### 64. L'Hôpital's rule

**Informal.** If f(a) = g(a) = 0, f and g are differentiable near a with g′ ≠ 0 away from a, and f′(x)/g′(x) → L as x → a, then f(x)/g(x) → L.

**Scope.** The `0/0` form at a point, for `(f g : ℝ → ℝ)`, with limits as the library's punctured limit `TendsToAt` and quotients as `pointwiseDiv`: from `f(A) = 0`, `g(A) = 0`, `g′ ≠ 0` away from `A`, and `TendsToAt(pointwiseDiv(f′, g′), L, A)` follows `TendsToAt(pointwiseDiv(f, g), L, A)`. Differentiability is assumed on an open ball around `A`, including `A`; the Cauchy mean value theorem `CauchyMeanValue.theorem`, `f′(C) * (g(B) - g(A)) = g′(C) * (f(B) - f(A))`, proved from Rolle's theorem, gives for each `x` on either side of `A` a point strictly between where `f/g` equals `f′/g′`. The `∞/∞` form and limits at infinity are not covered.

[Proof: LHopital.theorem](../lib/Grace/Cumulus/Real/Analysis/LHopital.g17#L269)

### 70. The perfect number theorem

**Informal.** If 2^p − 1 is prime then 2^(p−1)(2^p − 1) is perfect (Euclid), and every even perfect number has this form (Euler).

**Scope.** `sigma(n)` is the sum of the divisors of `n`, `natSum(divisors(n), identity(ℕ))` over the naturals, and `PerfectNumber(n)` says `0 < n ∧ sigma(n) = 2 * n`. `perfectNumber.of_mersenne` gives Euclid's direction for every `p` (primality of `2 ^ p - 1` already forces `p ≥ 2`), `perfectNumber.even_form` gives Euler's converse as `∃ p ∈ ℕ, Prime(2 ^ p - 1) ∧ n = 2 ^ (p - 1) * (2 ^ p - 1)`, and `perfectNumber.even_iff` combines them. Underneath: `sigma.multiplicative`, `sigma(m * n) = sigma(m) * sigma(n)` for `gcd(m, n) = 1`, proved through the bijection `(d, e) ↦ d · e` from `divisors(m) × divisors(n)` onto `divisors(m * n)` and the sum of products over a product set; `sigma.prime_iff`, `σ(p) = p + 1` characterises the primes above 1; and `sigma.two_pow`, `σ(2 ^ k) = 2 ^ (k + 1) - 1`, from `divisors(2 ^ (k + 1))` being 1 together with the doubles of `divisors(2 ^ k)`. Euler's direction writes `n = 2 ^ k · m` with `m` odd, reads `(2 ^ (k + 1) − 1) σ(m) = 2 ^ (k + 1) m`, and bounds the sum of the divisors strictly between 1 and `m`. Everything is in ZF.

**Scope.** For a finite group `G` (one set with carrier, operation, unit, and inverse) and a subgroup `H`, `cardinality(H)` divides `order(G)`, the size of the carrier, and `order(G) = index(G, H) · cardinality(H)` with the index the number of left cosets `leftCosets(G, H)`. The proof is the textbook one: the cosets `g • H` partition the group (`leftCoset.eq_or_disjoint`, `leftCosets.cover`), each is in bijection with `H` by the translation `h ↦ g * h` (`leftCoset.cardinality`), and a disjoint union of equal blocks is counted by `cardinality.big_union_constant`. Independently of finiteness, `g ↦ (gH, rep(gH)⁻¹ · g)` is a bijection from the carrier onto `leftCosets(G, H) × H` (`Group.equipotent_cosets_product`); its representative is chosen with Global Choice. Normal subgroups and quotient groups are developed in their own modules.

### 72. Sylow's theorem

**Informal.** A finite group of order p^k·m with p prime not dividing m has a subgroup of order p^k; every p-subgroup lies in a conjugate of it, any two Sylow p-subgroups are conjugate, and the number of Sylow p-subgroups divides m and is congruent to 1 modulo p.

**Scope.** Over a bundled group `Group(G)` with `Finite(G)`, `Prime(p)`, `¬(p ∣ m)`, and `order(G) = p ^ k * m`: `sylow.exists_subgroup` gives `∃ P, Subgroup(P, G) ∧ cardinality(P) = p ^ k`; `sylow.subset_conjugate` gives `∃ g ∈ G, Q ⊆ conjugateSet(G, g, P)` for every `PSubgroup(Q, G, p)` and `SylowSubgroup(P, G, p)`; `sylow.conjugate` gives `∃ g ∈ G, Q = conjugateSet(G, g, P)` for two Sylow p-subgroups; `sylow.count_dvd` and `sylow.count_mod` give `cardinality(sylowSubgroups(G, p)) ∣ m` and `cardinality(sylowSubgroups(G, p)) % p = 1`. The proofs use group actions `act : G ↷ X` with orbits, stabilizers, and the orbit–stabilizer theorem `cardinality(orbit(x)) * cardinality(stabilizer(x)) = order(G)`: existence by the translation action on the subsets of size p^k, whose number `(p ^ k * m) choose (p ^ k)` is prime to p by Lucas' congruence; conjugacy by a p-subgroup acting on the cosets of a Sylow subgroup; the count by a Sylow subgroup acting on the Sylow subgroups by conjugation, with the class equation and the normalizer alongside.

### 73. Ascending or descending sequences

**Informal.** Every sequence of mn + 1 distinct elements of a linear order has an increasing subsequence of length m + 1 or a decreasing one of length n + 1.

**Scope.** The Erdős–Szekeres theorem. For a bundled linear order `LinearOrder(P)` and an injective `f : m * n + 1 → points(P)`, there is an index set `S ∈ subsetsOfSize(m * n + 1, m + 1)` with `IncreasingOn(P, f, S)` or one in `subsetsOfSize(m * n + 1, n + 1)` with `DecreasingOn(P, f, S)`, where `IncreasingOn` says `f(i) ≤ f(j)` for indices `i < j` of `S`. The proof labels each index with the length of the longest increasing run and of the longest decreasing run ending there, the latter as a run of the dual order; a later larger term lengthens the first label and a later smaller term the second, so the label pairs are distinct, and there are only m·n of them.

### 88. Derangements formula

**Informal.** The number of permutations of n objects with no fixed point is d(n) = n! ∑_{k ≤ n} (−1)^k / k!, equivalently d(n) = ∑_{k ≤ n} (−1)^k (n choose k)(n − k)!, with d(n + 2) = (n + 1)(d(n + 1) + d(n)).

**Scope.** `derangements(I)` is the set of permutations of `I` without a fixed point. `derangements.natural_count` gives `cardinality(derangements(n)) = derangement(n)` for the recurrence-defined sequence 1, 0, 1, 2, 9, 44, …, `derangements.recurrence` the textbook recurrence for the counts themselves, and `derangements.formula` the inclusion–exclusion sum in the integers, `integerOfNat(cardinality(derangements(n))) = derangementSum(n, n + 1)` with `derangementSum(n, k + 1) = derangementSum(n, k) + (−1)^k · (n choose k) · (n − k)!`, the integer form of n! ∑ (−1)^k/k!. The count is proved by the classical bijection: a derangement sends a chosen point a to some b, and either returns b to a, leaving a derangement of the other n − 2 points, or not, where composing with the transposition (a b) and forgetting a leaves a derangement of n − 1 points. The formula follows from the one-step recurrence d(n + 1) = (n + 1)d(n) + (−1)^(n + 1).

### 89. The factor and remainder theorems

**Informal.** Dividing a polynomial p by X − a leaves the remainder p(a); a is a root exactly when X − a divides p.

**Scope.** For a polynomial over any commutative ring, given by its Horner coefficient list, `remainder.theorem` is the synthetic-division identity `p(x) = p(a) + (x − a)·q(x)` with `q` the synthetic quotient, so the remainder on division by `X − a` is `p(a)`; `factor.theorem` specialises it at a root to `p(x) = (x − a)·q(x)`, and `factor.root` recovers the root from such a factorisation. Polynomials are concrete coefficient lists evaluated by Horner's rule, not elements of an abstract polynomial ring.

### 93. The birthday problem

**Informal.** Among 23 people with independent, uniformly random birthdays in a year of 365 days, the probability that two share a birthday exceeds 1/2; for n people and d days it is 1 − d(d − 1)⋯(d − n + 1)/dⁿ.

**Scope.** An assignment of birthdays to the people of `A` from the days of `B` is a function `A → B`, and `sharedBirthday(A, B)` is the set of assignments outside `injections(A, B)`; `sharedBirthday.membership` reads it as the assignments under which two distinct people get the same day. `birthday.counting` is the arithmetic core, `2 * fallingFactorial(365, 23) < 365 ^ 23`, certified by the kernel's closed-term evaluator through the equation `2 · 365·364⋯343 + 1 + c = 365²³` for the explicit 58-digit gap `c`. `birthday.injections` transfers it to any finite sets of 23 people and 365 days through `injections.count` and `functionSpace.count`: fewer than half of the assignments are injective. `sharedBirthday.probability` is the general formula on the uniform probability space of all assignments, `prob(uniformWeight(functionSpace(A, B)), sharedBirthday(A, B)) = 1 − realNat(fallingFactorial(|B|, |A|)) / realNat(|B| ^ |A|)` for finite `A` and finite nonempty `B`, from the complement rule of the uniform distribution; `birthday.problem` specializes it and bounds the quotient below 1/2 from the counting fact, giving `1/2 < prob(…)` as a real inequality. Every theorem's axiom audit lists only ZF axioms.

### 97. Cramer's rule

**Informal.** A solution of a square linear system is given by ratios of determinants.

**Scope.** Over a bundled commutative ring `CommRing(R)`, for a square matrix `M : R ^ n × n` and a solution `x` of the row system `x · M = b` (`rowProduct(R, n, x, M)(j) = b(j)` for every column `j`), `det(M) * x(i) = det(N)` where `N` is `M` with row `i` replaced by `b`; no division is assumed, so the statement holds over every commutative ring and gives `x(i) = det(N) / det(M)` over a field. The adjugate identity `M * adj(M) = det(M) · 1` is stated in the matrix ring `matrixRing(R, n)`, and when `det(M)` has an inverse `u`, `M * scalarMultiple(R, n, u, adj(M)) = matrixRing(R, n)[1]`. The column form and the two-sided inverse await the invariance of the determinant under transposition. Everything is in ZF.

[Proof: Matrix.cramer](../lib/Grace/Cumulus/Algebra/Ring/MatrixCramer.g17#L143)

<details open>
<summary>Formal Grace17 statement</summary>

<!-- cumulus-source: lib/Grace/Cumulus/Algebra/Ring/MatrixCramer.g17 -->
```g17
theorem Matrix.cramer (M N : R ^ n × n) (x b i : Set)
  requires vector : x : n → R.carrier
  requires solution : ∀ j ∈ n, rowProduct(R, n, x, M)(j) = b(j)
  requires row : i ∈ n
  requires replaced : ∀ j ∈ n, N[i, j] = b(j)
  requires outside : ∀ k ∈ n, k ≠ i → ∀ j ∈ n, N[k, j] = M[k, j]
  shows det(M) * x(i) = det(N)
```
